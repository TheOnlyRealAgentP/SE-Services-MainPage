What is a septic system?

If your home is not connected to city sewer, everything that goes down your drains, including toilets, showers, sinks and laundry, flows to a septic system buried in your yard. The system holds the wastewater, lets the solids settle and break down, and returns cleaned water to the ground.

Most homes in East Harris County use one of two types: a conventional system, which treats the water in the soil, or an aerobic system, which treats it inside the tanks and sprays it on the lawn. Much of the area has dense clay soil that drains slowly, so aerobic systems are common here.

Type 1

Conventional septic system

A septic tank separates the solids, and a drain field lets the liquid soak into the soil, where it is filtered and treated naturally. This works best in soil that drains well.

  1. From the house

    All of the home's wastewater leaves through one pipe and flows downhill to the tank.

  2. Septic tank

    Heavy solids sink to the bottom as sludge, while grease and oils float to the top as scum. Bacteria break down part of the waste, and baffles keep the scum and sludge inside the tank.

  3. Distribution box

    The liquid from the middle of the tank, called effluent, flows to a box that splits it evenly between the drain lines.

  4. Drain field

    Perforated pipes in gravel trenches let the effluent seep into the soil, which filters and treats it before it reaches groundwater.

Type 2 · What we install

Three-tank aerobic system

An aerobic system treats the water inside the tanks using air and oxygen-loving bacteria, then disinfects it and sprays it on the lawn. It works well in heavy clay soil and on smaller lots. We install three-tank aerobic systems.

  1. Trash tank

    Wastewater enters the first tank, where solids settle to the bottom and grease floats to the top, just as in a conventional tank.

  2. Aeration chamber

    An air pump pushes air into the second tank. The oxygen feeds aerobic bacteria that break down waste faster and more completely than in a conventional tank.

  3. Clarifier

    In a calm section of the same tank, remaining particles settle back into the aeration chamber, leaving clear water at the top.

  4. Chlorinator

    The clear water passes over chlorine tablets that disinfect it on its way to the last tank.

  5. Pump tank

    The third tank holds the treated water. When it fills, a pump switches on.

  6. Spray heads

    The pump sends the treated water to sprinkler heads that spray it over the lawn, where it soaks in and evaporates.

How to keep your system working

A septic system can last for decades with simple care. Most expensive repairs start with something that went down a drain or a maintenance visit that was skipped.

{% include icon.html name="check" %} Do

  • Keep your maintenance contract and inspections current
  • Have the tanks pumped every two to three years, or sooner with heavy use
  • Spread laundry and other big water uses across the week
  • Fix running toilets and dripping faucets right away
  • Keep the chlorinator stocked with tablets made for wastewater systems, not pool tablets
  • Leave the air pump running and its breaker on
  • Keep the tank lids reachable and the spray area clear
  • Call when the alarm comes on, the sooner the better

{% include icon.html name="x" %} Don't

  • Flush wipes (even "flushable" ones), paper towels, feminine products, diapers or cat litter
  • Pour grease, cooking oil or food scraps down the drain
  • Pour paint, solvents, pesticides or harsh drain cleaners down any drain
  • Use large amounts of bleach or antibacterial cleaners at once
  • Flush leftover medications
  • Drive, park or build over the tanks, lines, drain field or spray area
  • Plant trees or large shrubs near the tanks and lines, where roots can break in
  • Send gutters, sump pumps or pool water into the system or onto the drain field

Warning signs to watch for

Catching a problem early usually means a smaller repair. Call us if you notice any of these.

  • {% include icon.html name="alert" %} The alarm comes on

    A light or buzzer on the control panel means a pump, the air pump or the water level needs attention.

  • {% include icon.html name="alert" %} Slow or gurgling drains

    Sinks, tubs and toilets that drain slowly or gurgle can mean a full tank or a blocked line.

  • {% include icon.html name="alert" %} Sewage odor

    A strong smell inside or in the yard often means the air pump has stopped or the tanks need pumping.

  • {% include icon.html name="alert" %} Soggy ground

    Standing water or wet spots near the tanks, drain field or spray area point to a leak or an overloaded system.

  • {% include icon.html name="alert" %} Weak sprinklers

    On an aerobic system, spray heads that barely spray can mean a broken line or a failing pump.

  • {% include icon.html name="alert" %} Backups

    Sewage coming up in a tub, shower or floor drain needs attention right away. Stop using water and call.
